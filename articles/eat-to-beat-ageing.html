How to eat to beat ageing

Discovering he is getting old before his time, David Cox sets out to lower his biological age by changing his diet in a helpful new book.

✍ Graham Lawton·📅 April 2026·⏱ 6 min read·📊 C1 Hard

Every good narrativea story or account of events needs an inciting incidentan event that starts the main story — something to kick-startmake something start moving quickly the protagonistthe main character in a story into action. For freelanceself-employed; not working for one company health journalist David Cox, it arrived in the form of a health scarea sudden frightening event, which he recountsdescribes or tells about an event in his new book, The Age Code: The new science of food and how it can save us.

In his mid-30s, increasingly immersed in the burgeoninggrowing rapidly field of gerosciencethe science of ageing, Cox decided to have his biological age measured. He took three tests, which all deliveredproduced; gave the same wake-up calla warning that makes you realise something serious: he was getting old before his time.

His biological age — a measure of the amount of age-related damage he had accumulatedbuilt up gradually over time in his 34 years — was what you would expect in an average 36-year-old. So no cause for panic, but, as he explains, "if I do absolutely nothing, projectionsestimates of what will happen in the future suggest that by the time I reach my fifties, I would be at a notably higher risk of developing a chronic(of an illness) lasting a long time illness such as diabetes or cancer." And so begins his absorbingvery interesting; holding your attention and often demandingrequiring a lot of effort questa long search for something to do just that.

I will deal with the elephant in the rooman obvious problem nobody wants to talk about right away. In principle, the notion of biological age is a sound concept — people age at different rates and there can be large mismatchesthings that don't fit together properly between an individual's chronological ageyour actual age in years and the amount of age-related damage they have accumulated. Biological clocks aim to deal with this mismatch in a simple and easy-to-understand number: your biological age. They also reflect the fact that age-related damage can be arrestedstopped from progressing or even reversed.

But in practice, they are flawedhaving faults or weaknesses for many reasons — not least that different clocks produce different numbers. But the fact that Cox took three tests and they all aligned, plus the fact that his goal was to reduce his biological age rather than just take a snapshot, means that we can give him the benefit of the doubt.

Cox quickly discovers that the easiest way to lower his biological age is to change his diet. At the start of his quest, he is a few kilograms overweight and his eating habits leave room for improvement, with too many sugary drinks, chocolate biscuits, desk-bound fast-food lunches and barely a wholegrainmade from the whole of a grain or legumea plant family including beans, peas and lentils in sight. His typical energy intakethe amount of something you take in (food, drink) is 2700 calories a day, higher than the 2500 calories that the NHS recommends for an average adult male.

Through talking to some of geroscience's leading lights, Cox discovers that this sub-optimalnot as good as it could be diet is opening him up to multiple driversthings that cause something to happen of ageing. Ten in all, in fact, starting with the most obvious: too many calories. Some of the others are also familiar, such as too little fibre, too few micronutrients and too much of the wrong sort of fat.

Other drivers he cites, however, are still quite obscurenot well known; difficult to understand outside the geroscience and nutrition literature. Many of them, such as dietary acid load and advanced glycation end products, have been the subject of New Scientist features. I was impressed with Cox's grip on the science and how to turn it into actionableable to be put into action self-improvement.

From an initial attempt to reduce his energy intake, Cox works through the drivers and chronicles his attempts to reduce his exposure to them. These feature an impressive number of visits to laboratories to have his body and its various fluids prodded, poked, extracted and measured.

He also, memorably, tries to eat more than 50 grams of fibre a day, which is way over the recommended 25 to 30 grams a day (a trend that is known as "fibremaxxing", though Cox doesn't use that term). I have done that too, and it is very difficult to actually ingesttake into the body by eating or swallowing that amount of fibre.
