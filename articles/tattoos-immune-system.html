The effect of tattoos on the body is more than skin deep

New research finds tattoo ink causes long-term changes in the immune system that may affect how your body fights off disease.

✍ Christa Lesté-Lasserre·📅 December 2025·⏱ 6 min read·📊 C1 Advanced

Tattoo ink collects in lymph nodes and interferes withprevents something from working properly the immune system, causing potentiallypossibly; depending on circumstances lifelonglasting for the whole of someone's life changes to the body's disease-fighting mechanismsthe natural processes by which something happens.

That is the conclusion of a study in mice, in which tattooed animals showed chronic(of an illness) lasting a long time inflammationswelling or irritation in the body, often a defence response in their lymph nodes — which were pigmented with the ink — and had altered antibodya substance the body makes to fight infection responses to vaccines. Human lymph nodes from tattooed individuals had similar inflammation and colouring.

"When you're tattooing, you're actually injecting ink into your body," says Santiago González at the University of Lugano in Switzerland. "It's not just a cosmeticrelated only to appearance, not health effect... there are effects on the immune system as well."

Tattooing has become a global phenomenona fact or situation that exists, especially one studied. Between 30 and 40 per cent of people in Europe and the US under the age of 40 have at least one tattoo.

González says he and his colleagues were working on an unrelated research project on inflammation in mice when they realised the animals developed "crazy inflammatory reactions" after being given small tattoos for identification.

To find out more, they used standard commercial inks in black, red and green to tattoo a 25-square-millimetre patcha small area, different from what is around it of skin on the hind feet of dozens of mice. With specialiseddesigned or trained for a specific purpose imaging equipment, they watched the ink travel along the lymphatic vesselstubes that carry fluid through the body's immune system inside the leg up to the nearby lymph nodes almost immediately.

There, the team saw that macrophagesimmune cells that absorb harmful particles in the body — immune cells that clean up debrisbroken or unwanted pieces left after damage, pathogenstiny organisms that cause disease and dead cells — captured the ink, tintinggiving something a slight colour the nodes and provokingcausing or starting a strong reaction acute(of illness or pain) severe but lasting only a short time inflammation.

Within about 24 hours, those macrophages died, releasing the ink, which then got captured by other macrophages. Those, too, would die and release ink, which would get taken up by yet other macrophages — creating a cycle of chronic inflammation that lasted well after the tattoo site had healed.

By the end of the experiment, two months after tattooing, the mice's lymph nodes still had levels of inflammatory markers up to five times higher than normal, says González.

To investigate whether this inflammation affected immune function, they injected vaccinesinjections that protect you from a disease directly into the tattooed skin. The tattooed mice's antibody response to a covid-19 mRNA vaccine was noticeably weaker than in control mice, but their response to an influenza vaccine was stronger.

Finally, they examined a small set of lymph node biopsiessmall samples of tissue taken from the body for testing from people who had been tattooed in regions near the nodes. Even two years after tattooing, the nodes still contained visible pigment, packed into the same kinds of macrophages as seen in the mouse study.

Further analyses showed the lymph node macrophages of tattooed mice were so full of ink, they captured less of the covid-19 vaccine — which, as an mRNA vaccine, needs processing by macrophages to be functional. For the protein-based influenza vaccine, however, inflammation boostedincreased or improved the antibody response, perhaps because there were more immune cells recruited to the tattooed site.
