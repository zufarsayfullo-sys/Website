Taking multivitamins may slightly slow rates of ageing

A new study suggests daily supplements could slow ageing — but experts caution the effect may be modest and not yet a recommendation for everyone.

✍ Michael Le Page·📅 April 2026·⏱ 5 min read·📊 C1 Hard

Taking a daily dose of vitamins and minerals has already been shown to slow cognitive declineloss of mental abilities like memory and thinking in people over 60. Now, it appears such supplementsextra vitamins or minerals taken to improve health could slow ageing more generally.

"We aren't yet at a point where it could be recommended that all older adults take multivitaminspills containing many vitamins and minerals," says Howard Sesso at Harvard University. That said, there may at least be small benefits with very little risk. "There's been no deleteriousharmful effects of a daily multivitamin that we've identified so far," he says.

Blood samples were taken from the participants at the start of the study, after one year and after two years. The DNA of immune cells in their blood was then analysed to look for the presence or absence of epigeneticrelated to how genes are switched on or off markersbiological signs that show something is happening — chemical tags that get added to DNA — at specific sites in the genome.

The patterns of epigenetic markers change in a predictablehappening in a way you can expect way as people age, allowing researchers to roughly estimatemake an approximate calculation how old they are from blood tests. A number of different epigenetic clocks of this kind have been developed, which vary based on which sites on the genome they look at.

Sesso's team used five epigenetic clockstools that estimate biological age from DNA changes, which all suggested that the people taking multivitamins aged slightly less than those on placebo, but the results were only significant for two of the clocks. "The statistically significant results were for the second-generation clocks that other groups have shown to be the most reliable and sensitive for evaluating longevitylong life interventionsactions taken to change a situation," says Horvath, who develops epigenetic clocks.

The first-generation clocks are good at predicting a person's age, but many of the epigenetic markers they look at aren't directly relevant to health, says Horvath. The second-generation clocks are based on markers linked to deterioratingbecoming worse health and the risk of dying. "I should note, however, that the effect sizes are modestnot very large; small but real," he says. "This is not a fountain of youtha mythical source of eternal youth."

The researchers claim that the slowing of epigenetic clocks they found is equivalent to about four months over the two-year study period, but this may be misleading. One of the many issues with epigenetic clocks is that their estimates of how a slowing of the same magnitude translates intoresults in; turns into normal time vary widely between studies, says Daniel Belsky at Columbia University in New York.
