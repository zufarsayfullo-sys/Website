Fresh insight into early human mating

Genetic evidence hints that Neanderthal and human interbreeding was strongly sex-biased — but the reason why is a mystery.

✍ Michael Marshall·📅 March 2026·⏱ 7 min read·📊 C1 Advanced

When our species and Neanderthals interbredhad children together (between two species or groups), it may have been mostly female Homo sapiens and male Neanderthals who mated.

It isn't clear why this sex-biasedfavouring one sex over the other mating pattern happened. It may be that male Neanderthals preferred female H. sapiens over females of their own species, or that female H. sapiens preferred Neanderthal males, or both. There is also no way to determine whether the matings were consensualagreed to by all the people involved or forced.

"I think we can say very little," says Alexander Platt at the University of Pennsylvania in Philadelphia. "The meaningful thing that we can say is that it... took place over generations."

Other geneticists say the evidence is intriguingvery interesting because of being mysterious but inconclusivenot leading to a clear answer. "I think we need more evidence, because it's a big claim about the behaviour," says Arev Sümer at the Max Planck Institute for Evolutionary Anthropology in Leipzig, Germany.

We have known since 2010 that H. sapiens — sometimes referred to as modern humans — and Neanderthals interbred after some members of our species moved out of Africa into Eurasia. There seem to have been multiple periods of interbreedinghaving children with members of another group or species: one around 50,000 to 43,000 years ago, and an earlier one that is harder to pin down, perhaps 200,000 years or more in the past. Today, all people of non-African descent carry some Neanderthal DNA.

However, researchers haven't paid much attention to how this affected the sex chromosomes. Females typically have two X chromosomes, while males usually have one X and one Y. Platt and his colleagues Sarah Tishkoff and Daniel Harris, also at the University of Pennsylvania, focused on the human and Neanderthal X chromosomes.

"One of the things that's been pretty abundantly clearvery obvious; extremely clear when looking at the human X chromosome is that it's almost entirely a Neanderthal desert," says Harris. Compared with other chromosomes, the human X chromosome is virtually devoidalmost completely without of Neanderthal DNA. The team considered four reasons for this.

First, it could be H. sapiens and Neanderthals were sufficiently different that their DNA didn't work together very well. This is called hybrid incompatibilitythe state of being unable to work or exist together, and could cause human-Neanderthal hybridsoffspring from two different species or groups to have difficulties with health or reproduction. However, the team found that the Neanderthal X chromosome contained more H. sapiens DNA than did the Neanderthal non-sex chromosomes — suggesting the X-chromosome DNA from the two hominins was compatibleable to work or exist together without problems.

Second, natural selectionthe process where organisms better adapted to their environment survive could have favoured modern human DNA. Neanderthals had quite small populations, so natural selection would have struggled to eliminatecompletely remove or get rid of harmful mutationschanges in DNA that damage health. In contrast, modern humans had larger populations and bad mutations would have been cleared, so modern human X-chromosome DNA would have spread in the Neanderthal population. However, the team says that doesn't make sense either, because the modern human DNA retained on the Neanderthal X chromosome is mostly in non-functionalnot having a useful purpose or job regions, where it wouldn't do any good.

Alternatively, there could be a cultural reason. Societies differ in how the sexes move around: in some, females leave their home group to live with their sexual partners, but in others, it is the males who relocatemove to a new place. Modern human women moving to live with Neanderthals could have produced a bias on the X chromosome, but it wouldn't be enough to account forexplain or be the reason for something the strength of the bias found.

That, the team says, leaves just one explanation: a mating preferencethe act of liking one thing more than another. Male Neanderthals preferred female H. sapiens over Neanderthal partners, or female H. sapiens preferred male Neanderthals over human partners — or both. "If they just like it that way, that explains everything," says Platt.

However, other geneticists say we cannot be so confident the alternative explanations are wrong. Sümer points out that the earlier episode of interbreeding had a drasticextreme; having a strong effect effect on the Neanderthal genome: their original Y chromosomes were completely replaced by H. sapiens Y chromosomes.

We can't rule outdecide that something is not possible hybrid incompatibility either, says Moisès Coll Macià at the Institute of Evolutionary Biology in Barcelona, Spain. The team assumed it would be equally intense when Neanderthal DNA entered the modern human genome and vice versa. "That might not be the case," he says.

Coll Macià says we also need to consider another possibility: meiotic drive. Roguebehaving in an unusual and uncontrolled way genetic elements can cause one of a pair of chromosomes to be passed on more often than would be expected by chance. His team has found tentativeuncertain; not yet proven evidence of this happening in H. sapiens outside Africa, resulting in the deletion of Neanderthal DNA from their X chromosomes.
