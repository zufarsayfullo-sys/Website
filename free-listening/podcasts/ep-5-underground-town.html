Life against the odds in Australia's underground town

BBC Reel visits Coober Pedy in South Australia — where 60% of residents live underground and the town is becoming a global leader in renewable energy.

BBC Reporter: Coober Pedy is at the centre of Australia's opal mining industry. But this remote town in the South Australian outback is famous for something else — 60% of its residents live underground.

The reason comes down to the climate. Summer temperatures can reach 50 degrees Celsius on the surface. To escape the scorching heat, the town's early residents — mostly miners — began carving their homes directly into the hillsides. These underground homes, known as dugouts, stay at a constant, comfortable temperature year round, without the need for air conditioning.

A typical dugout has bedrooms, a kitchen, a bathroom and a lounge — all excavated from the rock. Natural insulation means that even on the hottest days, it can be cool and pleasant inside.

But Coober Pedy's ingenuity doesn't stop underground. The town is now becoming a leader in sustainable, renewable energy. A hybrid solar and wind power system now provides around 70% of the town's electricity, reducing its dependence on expensive diesel generators that previously had to transport fuel hundreds of kilometres across the outback.

Town representative: We're in the middle of nowhere, and we've gone from being completely off-grid and dependent on diesel to generating most of our power from the sun and wind. We want to show the world that if we can do it here — in one of the harshest environments on the planet — anyone can do it.

BBC Reporter: With a population of around 2,000 people from over 45 different nationalities, Coober Pedy has always been a community built on resilience and resourcefulness. Today, it's showing the world how even the most remote, arid places can lead the way in sustainable living.
